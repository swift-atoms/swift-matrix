# swift-matrix

Use this package when you need a fixed-size, stack-stored `Rows × Columns` grid of scalars with the dimensions checked at compile time: matrix products, applying a matrix to a `Vector<Columns, Scalar>`, transposition, the matrix form of a `Scale`, `Shear`, `Rotation`, `Quaternion` or `Projection`, affine maps of the plane as `Matrix<2, 3, Scalar>` (translations, scales and turns about a point, and the map nearest to point correspondences), and translations, scales and turns about the axes of space as `Matrix<4, 4, Scalar>`. Start from `Matrix<Rows, Columns, Scalar>`. It carries no coordinate space; when the matrix is a linear map between tagged spaces, use `Linear.Matrix` from swift-linear, which wraps this type.

## Laws

- Storage is row-major: `rows[row][column]` is the entry `self[row, column]`, and `multiplied(by:)` and `applied(to:)` treat vectors as columns. `a.multiplied(by: b)` is the product `a · b`, so `a.multiplied(by: b).applied(to: v) == a.applied(to: b.applied(to: v))` in exact arithmetic. With floating-point scalars the two sides can differ by rounding.
- Every product entry starts at `.zero` and accumulates the terms in increasing inner index, one addition at a time. Floating-point results therefore match that left-to-right sum exactly, including its rounding.
- A product over a zero inner dimension is the zero matrix, and a product with a zero outer dimension keeps its shape. `transposed` applied twice returns the original matrix.
- Equality and hashing are entry by entry, and all matrices with no entries are equal.
- `Matrix(scale)` is diagonal with the scale's factors on the diagonal and exact zeros elsewhere. Non-finite factors are copied unchanged. `Matrix(first.concatenating(second)) == Matrix(second).multiplied(by: Matrix(first))`.
- `Matrix(shear)` is the identity plus the shear's factor at `[target, source]`. Composing two shears multiplies their matrices; it does not add their off-diagonal factors.
- `Matrix(rotation)` starts from the identity and applies `rotation.planes` in order. Each plane rotates its `first` axis toward its `second` for a positive angle. `Matrix(a.followed(by: b))` equals `Matrix(b).multiplied(by: Matrix(a))` up to rounding, and the result is orthonormal up to rounding. The conversion forgets parameters: a full-turn rotation gives a matrix that is approximately the identity even though the rotation is not `.identity`.
- With the `Quaternion` trait, `Matrix<3, 3, Scalar>(quaternion)` is the rotation matrix of a unit `Quaternion` from swift-quaternion, by the unit formula, so a quaternion that is not of unit length gives a matrix that is not a rotation. `Matrix(a.multiplied(by: b))` equals `Matrix(a).multiplied(by: Matrix(b))`, and `Matrix(q.conjugate)` equals `Matrix(q).transposed`, up to rounding. `Quaternion(matrix)` is the unit quaternion of a rotation matrix by Shepperd's method, accurate for every rotation, half turns included, with a non-negative real part, so `Quaternion(Matrix(q))` is `q` or `-q` up to rounding. A matrix that is not a rotation gives a quaternion with no meaning. To turn between two rotation matrices at an even angular rate, convert both and use `Quaternion.interpolated(to:at:)`.
- `Matrix(projection)` is the 4 × 4 matrix whose rows are `projection.coefficients`, acting on homogeneous column vectors (x, y, z, 1). `Matrix(projection).applied(to:)` of such a vector equals `projection.clip(_:)` of the point exactly, because both sum each row from zero in column order. The last row is (0, 0, −1, 0) for a perspective projection and (0, 0, 0, 1) for an orthographic one. A projection composes with a view transform by an ordinary product: `Matrix(projection).multiplied(by: view)` projects what `view` has moved into eye space.
- `Matrix.identity` of a square matrix has ones on the diagonal and exact zeros elsewhere, and is neutral for the product on both sides.
- A `Matrix<2, 3, Scalar>` is an affine map of the plane: the 2 × 2 `linear` part in columns 0 and 1 and the `translation` in column 2. `applied(to:)` of a `Vector<2, Scalar>` reads it as the point (x, y, 1) and equals `applied(to:)` of that three-component column exactly. A direction, which no translation moves, goes through `linear.applied(to:)`.
- For two such maps `a.multiplied(by: b)` applies `b` first, like every other product here: it equals the top two rows of the product of the two 3 × 3 matrices whose last row is (0, 0, 1). `Matrix<2, 3, Scalar>.identity` is neutral on both sides, and `translation(u).multiplied(by: translation(v))` is `translation(u + v)`.
- `Matrix(linear:translation:)` builds the affine map from any 2 × 2 matrix, so a rotation, scale or shear about the origin is `Matrix(linear: Matrix(rotation))`, and about another point it is a product with two translations.
- `Matrix(linear:about:)` is the 2 × 2 map about a point: its translation is the point less the map applied to the point, so the point stays where it is and the map equals `translation(c) · Matrix(linear:) · translation(−c)`. `scale(_:about:)` is that for the factors on the diagonal, one for each axis or one for both, about the origin by default, so `scale(f, about: c)` has the translation `c − f · c` exactly.
- A `Matrix<4, 4, Scalar>` that moves points of space is built from a 3 × 3 linear part and a translation by `Matrix(linear:translation:)`, with the last row (0, 0, 0, 1); `translation(_:)` and `scale(_:)` are that for the identity and for factors on the diagonal. Applied to (x, y, z, 1) it moves a point, and to (x, y, z, 0) a direction, which no translation moves.
- With the `Trigonometry` trait alone, which brings in swift-trigonometry and not swift-rotation, angles in radians give turns through the scalar's own `sin` and `cos`: `Matrix<2, 2, Scalar>.rotation(_:)` moves x toward y for a positive angle, `Matrix<2, 3, Scalar>.rotation(_:about:)` is it about a point, and `Matrix<4, 4, Scalar>.rotation(aboutX:)`, `rotation(aboutY:)` and `rotation(aboutZ:)` turn y toward z, z toward x and x toward y. Each equals `Matrix` of the `Rotation` of the same plane by the same angle, up to rounding.
- `inverted()` of a 2 × 2 matrix divides each entry of the adjugate by the `determinant`; of an affine map it is the inverse linear part with the translation it implies, so `m.inverted().multiplied(by: m)` is the identity up to rounding. Both throw `Matrix.Error.singular` exactly when the determinant is zero. The test is an exact comparison: a nearly singular matrix still inverts, and a determinant that is not a number is not refused.
- `Matrix<2, 3, Scalar>(fitting:onto:)` is the affine map nearest to taking each of `Count` source points to its target, the counts equal by type: it minimises the sum over the pairs of the squared Euclidean distance from the moved source to its target. Its linear part is the targets' covariance with the sources, both about their means, times the inverse of the sources' scatter about their mean, and it takes the sources' mean to the targets' mean; so the misses sum to zero. Three points not on a line fit exactly, and the four corners of a unit square, `[(0, 0), (1, 0), (1, 1), (0, 1)]`, or of any rectangle, fit the corners of a parallelogram exactly; for any other quadrilateral the fit is the least-squares one, not a projective map. Translating every target by v gives `translation(v)` times the fit, and translating every source by u gives the fit times `translation(−u)`, up to rounding. It throws `Matrix.Error.singular` exactly when the scatter's determinant is zero: sources all on one line, all at one point, or none.
